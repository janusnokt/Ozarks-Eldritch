The town sits on a font.

Dozens of springs once ran wild here. The town built itself on the promise of that sweetwater. The springs sang. Then they went quiet.

Water in karst country never vanishes; it goes down, through swallets and streams into the Beneath, and surfaces again somewhere of its own choosing. The founders saw water rising from the stone and called the place blessed.

Beneath their feet, the dark kept drinking.

Walk with the Fae

Follow the water. Mind the thresholds.
Give the ordinary world a little room to turn strange.

Janus and Amara Nokt lead a small group through the springs, the stones, the stairways, and through the seams between them. The route touches places where the old stories still hold their shape, and a few where something has kept the shape without the story.

Along the way: the courtesies and taboos of the fae, the etiquette of a threshold, and the rumor the Ozarks call the Sight. Storytelling opens into gentle, optional practices of attention. You choose how far to go.

  1. Hear the old stories.

    Thresholds, invitations, bargains, and the company you might keep once you leave the well-lit street.

  2. Practice noticing.

    Stand beside water and stone. Listen for the layer under the traffic. Soften your gaze and let a place look back.

  3. Carry something home.

    A story, a question, a different way of seeing. We close the walk together and return to the everyday with time to gather ourselves.

Questions people ask

Do I need to believe in the fae? In faerie tales?

No. Bring belief, disbelief, curiosity, skepticism, or nothing at all. The walk does not require you to accept anyone else’s cosmology. We work with folklore, attention, place, imagination, and the old human habit of finding meaning where water touches stone.

What will I be asked to do?

Walk. Listen. Notice. At several points we may invite you to use simple practices of attention: peripheral vision, contemplative listening, imagination or light entrancement. You remain awake, aware, and free to decline anything.

Is this a ghost tour?

Not quite. Eureka Springs has ghosts enough, but this walk travels older roads. We follow springs, thresholds, second sight, strange geography, and stories about the beings people once called the Good Folk, the Hidden People or the Kindly Neighbors.

Will I actually see a faerie?

People have claimed to… yet we make no promise of supernatural encounters. Some people experience these practices as deep seeing, some as heightened perception, some as spirituality, and some simply as a beautiful way of seeing an unusual town. It’s more interesting to see what happens when we leave the answer open.

What is “second sight”?

Across cultures, people have developed ways of seeing beyond ordinary, task-driven attention. For us, learning about the “second sight” means learning to notice differently: movement at the edge of vision, patterns in sound, the character of a place, the things your mind usually dismisses before they fully arrive.

Is hypnosis involved?

Not the hypnosis you have seen onstage. The Nokts specialize in gentle entrancement: focused attention, story, rhythm, imagination, and sensory awareness. You never surrender control. You can speak, move, stop, laugh, ask questions or simply observe.

Who are Janus and Amara Nokt?

The Nokts are writers, storytellers, and practitioners of entrancement whose work lives where folklore, altered attention, myth, and place meet. The aim is not to convince you what the world is. It is to help you notice how much of it ordinarily passes unseen.

Why Eureka Springs?

Because Eureka Springs already behaves like a story. Springs vanish beneath limestone. Streets fold over one another. Stairways climb where roads refuse to go. Hotels perch above ravines. Water disappears underground and returns elsewhere. The town makes an unusually good place to talk about thresholds.

How difficult is the walk?

Eureka Springs does not believe in level ground, yet this walk is relatively flat. Expect uneven pavement, and periods of standing. This is a walking experience rather than a seated performance. If mobility concerns might affect you, contact us before booking and we can tell you exactly what the current route involves.

Is it scary?

Sometimes uncanny. Occasionally dark. Never frightening in the conventional haunted-house sense. No jump scares or costumes. We offer wonder, beauty, strangeness, and the sensation that the familiar world has developed another depth.

What should I bring?

Comfortable shoes, weather-appropriate clothing, water, and curiosity. A small flashlight may be useful, though we sometimes deliberately work with low light.

What if the weather turns bad?

Light weather can become part of the atmosphere. Dangerous weather does not. If conditions make the walk unsafe, we will reschedule or provide the appropriate booking option.

Can children come?

The experience was designed primarily for adults and older teens. Younger guests may enjoy the stories, but some of the language, darkness, walking, and contemplative exercises may not hold their attention.

Is this a religious event?

No. We draw from folklore, mythology, historical beliefs, and contemplative practices, but you will not be asked to profess a belief, pray, worship or adopt a spiritual system.

What are the Kindly Neighbors?

One of the old polite names for the beings English-speaking folklore often calls faeries or the fae. Other names include the Little People, or the Shining Ones. “Faerie” has grown small in the modern imagination. The older stories did not always describe them that way. They could appear beautiful, dangerous, generous, alien, playful, or impossible to classify.

Why call them the Kindly Neighbors?

Because old folklore often treated names as invitations. People spoke carefully about what might be listening.

We choose to be polite.

The mythology of Ozarks Eldritch

The Kindly
Neighbors

You may have passed one without knowing. A figure beyond the rain-streaked window. A pale movement in the trees above the trail. The sudden certainty that a quiet spring has listened to every word you said.

They dwelt beneath these hills before the first bathhouse rose, and they never left. The old folk of spring and hollow, patient as limestone, hungry for whatever the road brings. New faces. Fresh stories. The wishes people carry down to the water. Eureka Springs keeps them fed, and they keep the town strange in return.

Some offer welcome. Some test your manners. A few seem to remember you from a visit you have yet to make.

The ones who notice
get noticed back.

Entrancement

Turn your attention inward.
Landscapes wait there, too.

Among the arts the Kindly Neighbors practice, one travels well: the loosening of the ordinary mind so that something else can see through it. Old hypnotists called it trance. Here it keeps the older name.

Every sitting begins with a conversation about what draws you to the work. From there the experience shapes itself around your interests and your comfort. Ask, pause, or stop at any moment; the entrancement answers to you.

  • A suggestion, carried to your room

    A private session at your hotel or rental. You choose what the suggestion should do after you wake: sleep deeper, dream of the springs, climb the town’s stairs without your knees complaining. It stays with you after we leave.

    Pricing and availability by enquiry.

  • Opening the Sight

    A course in noticing, taught across several sittings. The Neighbors show themselves to the ones who look properly. This teaches the looking.
